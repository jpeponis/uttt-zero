uttt-zero · an AlphaZero-style agent on one desktop

The game that plays you back

In Ultimate Tic-Tac-Toe, every move you make chooses the board your opponent must answer in. Nobody has solved this game. This is the story of teaching one desktop PC to play it very well starting from nothing — and of the harder problem underneath: knowing that it worked, and what it discovered about the game.

Part 1 · The game

Nine boards, and a steering wheel

You already know tic-tac-toe, and you already know its flaw: two people who pay attention always draw. It is a solved game — solved, in practice, by most ten-year-olds.

Now put nine tic-tac-toe boards in a 3×3 grid. Winning three small boards in a row wins the game. That alone would just be nine boring games in a trench coat. The rule that changes everything is this: the cell you pick inside a small board decides which board your opponent must play in next. Play the top-right cell of any board, and your opponent is sent to the top-right board.

Sit with that for a second, because it is the whole personality of the game. Every move is two moves: a mark, and a command. You never simply ask “what’s my best square?” — you also ask “where does this square send them?” A locally brilliant move can be a globally terrible gift, delivering your opponent to exactly the battlefield they wanted. Playing well feels less like tic-tac-toe and more like steering.

These are the rules of the CodinGame arena, a long-running online competition where people write programs to play games against each other. That last rule — the board count as a tiebreak — is no footnote. When two strong players meet, roughly a third of their games never produce a line of boards at all. Those games are decided by the count. Any honest analysis of this game has to treat “collect more boards” as a second way to win, not an afterthought.

The questions nobody can answer

Once you have played a few games, two questions become almost physically itchy. What is actually the best first move? Players argue. Centre of the centre board is the folk favourite, but corner openings have their partisans. And bigger: with perfect play, who wins? Ordinary tic-tac-toe is a draw. Is this one? Nobody knows. The game is unsolved.

Here is a crude way to feel the size of it. Each of the 81 cells can be empty, X or O, so there are at most 381 ways to colour the board — about 4 × 1038. That over-counts, because most colourings can never arise in a real game, but the honest number is still far beyond anything a computer can list. The most serious solving attempt to date is a heavily optimised exact search. On a 12-core desktop it needs hours to crack a position that is already about 20 moves into the game. Its author estimates that solving the whole game from the empty board would take a few hundred million CPU-hours — millions of dollars of raw computing.

So the question this project set out to answer is the more interesting one: if you cannot solve a game, how do you find out what’s true about it? How would you learn its best openings? What a move is worth? When games are decided? And how would you know you weren’t fooling yourself? Everything on this page is a piece of that answer.

Part 2 · Thinking ahead

You already know how a game computer works

Forget computers for a moment. How do you decide a move in any game? You mentally play it forward: “If I go here, they’ll go there, then I can go here… no, wait, that loses. What if I go here instead?” That inner monologue is the entire foundation of game-playing programs. Let’s make it just slightly more precise, on a game small enough to hold in your head.

Concept · the value of a position

Give every finished game a score from one player’s point of view: +1 if they won, 0 for a draw, −1 if they lost. Then work backwards: an unfinished position is worth whatever it ends up as when both sides play their best from here on. On your turn, you pick the move leading to the highest-value position; on their turn, assume they do the same for themselves — which means the worst for you.

So why isn’t every game solved this way? Size. In Ultimate Tic-Tac-Toe you usually have up to 9 choices, and up to 81 on a free move. A game can run 81 moves. The tree of “I go here, they go there” multiplies into more branches than anyone — or anyone’s computer — can visit. Exhaustive lookahead drowns a few moves in.

But notice what you do about that. You don’t consider every legal move. Most look silly at a glance, and you never read them at all. You look ahead along a handful of promising lines, and when a line runs deep you stop calculating and judge the resulting position by feel: “this looks good for me.” Two abilities are doing all the work. One is a shortlist: which moves are even worth considering? The other is a glance-evaluation: how good does this position look? Neither is calculation. Both are intuition, built from experience. To make a computer play a game it cannot solve, give it those two intuitions.

Concept · a neural network (all you need for now)

A neural network is a big formula with a great many adjustable dials, wired so that turning the dials changes its outputs smoothly. Ours takes a board position in and produces exactly the two intuitions above. The value is one number saying how good this position looks for the player about to move. The policy is a plausibility rating for each of the 81 cells — the shortlist. “Training” means showing the network positions where you know what the answers should have been, and nudging the dials so its outputs drift toward those answers. That is genuinely the whole idea: a very flexible formula fitted to examples, like a curve of best fit with a million knobs. That much is all the next three parts need. Part 6 opens the box a little, at the moment when the size of the formula becomes something we can change.

Now combine intuition with lookahead. Instead of every branch, explore the shortlist. Instead of reading each line to the bitter end, stop after a while and let the glance-value stand in for the verdict. And — the clever part — keep returning to the branches whose verdicts keep coming back good, so effort flows toward the lines that earn it. This family of methods is called Monte Carlo tree search.

One round of it has a name that will recur on every later part of this page. Walk down one promising line, ask the network for a verdict where you stop, carry that verdict back up to the move it came from: that is one simulation. When this page says an agent “thinks with 64 simulations per move”, it means the agent does that walk 64 times, then commits to the move that came out best. (This project uses a modern variant called Gumbel MCTS, which stays reliable with remarkably few simulations. Its details do not matter here.)

The property that matters is this: a search leaning on the network’s intuition plays a bit better than the raw intuition alone. Checking a few lines catches some of your gut’s mistakes. Hold onto that sentence. It is about to become an engine.

Part 3 · The idea

The staircase that builds itself

There’s a chicken-and-egg problem hiding in Part 2. The search needs a decent intuition to guide it. Where does the intuition come from? The obvious answer: from watching experts. In 2016, DeepMind’s AlphaGo did exactly that. Its networks were first trained on records of strong human Go play, then sharpened by practice. It went on to defeat Lee Sedol, one of the greatest Go players in history, in a match that stunned the game’s whole culture. Go had been expected to resist computers for another decade. It fell to intuition-plus-search.

Then, in 2017, came the twist that makes this field beautiful. AlphaGo Zero threw the human games away. No expert data, no opening books, no handcrafted wisdom — nothing but the rules, and a network whose dials start as random noise. It became stronger than the version taught by humanity. Here is the loop that makes starting from zero possible:

Two words from that figure will follow us to the end of the page. One trip around the loop — a batch of self-play games, then a round of training on them — is what the code calls an iteration. This page calls it a lap. A training run is a few hundred laps, one after another. And after every lap, the network’s dials are saved to disk. That saved copy is a checkpoint — a snapshot of the network at that moment. Every number about a run on this page was measured on one of those snapshots, and any snapshot can be loaded and played against any other.

Why doesn’t this loop just chase its own tail? Because of the sentence from Part 2: search amplifies intuition. At every stage, checking a few lines of play catches some mistakes. That is true even at the very first lap, when the network’s opinions are pure static. So the searched player is always a little stronger than the raw one, and that gap is the fuel. Training closes the gap from below. The amplifier immediately re-opens it from above, one step higher.

The gap never closes for good. In the finished AlphaGo Zero, the full player with search was rated about 5,185 on the Elo scale. Its own raw network, playing without search, measured about 3,055. The amplifier was still worth some two thousand rating points at the end of training. (Elo is a rating scale we’ll build properly in Part 5. For now: two thousand points is a chasm.)

What the papers are quieter about is the cost. AlphaGo Zero’s longer run played 29 million games against itself over 40 days on Google’s custom hardware. Its successor AlphaZero, which mastered chess and shogi the same way, used five thousand of Google’s custom chips just to generate its self-play games. This is a datacenter recipe.

uttt-zero is that recipe on one desktop. An RTX 3090 graphics card runs the loop. A second, smaller card, an RTX 3060, runs analysis. A 12-core CPU solves endgames exactly. And the project points the recipe somewhere the datacenter versions mostly didn’t: not just at producing a strong player, but at interrogating one — using the trained agent as an instrument for finding out what is true about the game. That required two things the rest of this page is about. The loop had to become fast enough to be a laboratory (Part 4). And the measurements had to be disciplined enough to keep the findings honest (Part 5 onward).

Part 4 · The engineering

The kitchen that mostly waited

Here is the mental model you need for the hardware, and it’s a restaurant. A GPU — a graphics card — is an enormous kitchen: thousands of line cooks, individually unremarkable, who can all chop in parallel, which makes the kitchen spectacular at one thing — preparing four thousand identical dishes at once. The Python program is the head chef, who does no cooking and only sends in orders. Every order, however tiny, costs the same fixed overhead to pass through the window. And sometimes the chef stops everything and stands at the window, waiting for a result to come back before deciding what to order next.

The first version of this project ran its lookahead as thousands of tiny orders. Profiling — timing every step of one simulation of the search — found 550 separate orders sent to the GPU, and 45 full stops where Python waited for an answer before continuing. The result: 93 % of the wall-clock time was spent asking, not cooking. A top-of-the-line GPU, mostly idle, drumming its fingers between micro-orders. Self-play crawled along at 25–33 games per second. The first full training run took twelve hours.

The fix was a ladder of five changes, each moving work away from that order window, in order of payoff:

  1. 1Stop waiting at the window Restructure the search so Python almost never needs an answer back mid-simulation — the chef keeps ordering without pausing. full stops per simulation: 45 → 2
  2. 2Batch the bookkeeping Keep the statistics of all four thousand parallel game trees in a few big tables, updated in single bulk operations instead of thousands of small ones. tree work per simulation: 17.8 → 12.4 ms
  3. 3Pre-record the whole order sequence The 550 orders are identical every simulation — so record them once (the GPU feature is called a “CUDA graph”) and replay the recording as a single order. 66 → 7.4 ms per simulation under load
  4. 4Streamline the network itself Fold constant arithmetic into the dials ahead of time, compute in half-precision numbers, lay memory out the way the GPU’s tensor hardware wants it. 16.8 → 11.6 ms per 4,096 positions evaluated
  5. 5Never let a seat go empty In naive lock-step self-play, finished games idle while the longest game in the batch drags on; instead, swap a fresh game in the moment one ends. no idle tail — the batch stays full

25 → 220 games per second

The GPU went from waiting to 84 % busy, and a full training run fell from a 12-hour commitment to a 2.3-hour experiment. That last number is the real prize: at 2.3 hours you can afford to test ideas one at a time and be wrong cheaply. Wall-clock stopped being the project’s constraint. What replaced it is the subject of the next section — and it turned out to be the harder problem.

Part 5 · The measurement

How do you avoid fooling yourself?

Suppose you change something in the recipe and train a new agent. Is it better than the old one? The obvious plan: play the two against each other and count wins. That kind of match — two specific agents, many games, one score — is what this page calls a head-to-head. Nearly everything about the obvious plan is a trap. Finding the traps before believing any results is the most quietly important work in the project.

Trap one: the coin is bent. In this game the first player, X, has a real advantage. Put two equally strong agents against each other at the usual thinking budget, and X wins 57–60 % of the games. So whoever happens to sit as X more often looks stronger without being stronger. The fix is to make every match paired. Each test opening is played twice, once with each agent as X, and only the pair’s combined score counts. The X advantage cancels out of every comparison by construction.

Trap two: “beats it 55 % of the time” doesn’t travel. A win rate compares two specific agents. To place many agents on one scale — including agents that never played each other — you want a common currency. Chess solved this decades ago with the Elo rating, and it’s worth one minute to actually own the idea rather than nod at it.

Concept · Elo, from scratch

Elo is nothing more than a convenient way to write down expected scores. Start by scoring a game as 1 for a win, ½ for a draw, 0 for a loss. Now declare that a rating gap of d points means the stronger side is expected to take 1 / (1 + 10−d/400) of the points. Try d = 100: 10−0.25 ≈ 0.562, so the expected share is 1 / 1.562, which is about 64 %. That is the entire definition: a dictionary between “points of rating” and “share of points won”. The 400 in the formula is chosen so that gaps add. Rather than juggling “A takes 64 % off B, and B takes 64 % off C”, you say A is +100 over B and +200 over C, and every comparison lives on one number line.

Trap three: 1,032 games is still luck-prone. Play the same match twice and the score wobbles. How much of any measured difference is real?

Concept · a confidence interval

Imagine replaying the whole measurement in a thousand parallel universes: same agents, same procedure, fresh luck each time. The scores would scatter. A 95 % confidence interval is the range that the middle 95 % of those universes land in. We can’t visit parallel universes, but we can fake them. Take the 516 opening pairs, draw 516 of them at random with replacement (so some pairs appear twice and some not at all), and score that alternative suite. Do it a thousand times. Each draw is a plausible version of the suite you might have had, and the spread of their scores is the interval. The trick is called the bootstrap. For the full suite it says a score is trustworthy to about ±2.8 points. Whenever this page gives an interval, that is what it is: the range where the middle 95 % of the replays land.

Trap four — the deepest one: the ruler itself wobbles. Three separate things add noise before a single game of the test is played. Train the identical recipe twice from different random starting dials, and the two “identical” agents differ by a couple of suite points. Compare two snapshots of one training run taken minutes apart, and they can differ by up to 4 points. Even re-running the same match in a fresh process shifts the score by ±0.4. (The GPU’s fast arithmetic is not perfectly reproducible. A single rounding that lands differently can change one move, and one move can change one game.) Two more instruments pin the scale to something outside the project’s own bubble. The first is an exact endgame set. It holds 3,000 late-game positions that are simple enough for the CPU solver to work out the perfect answer by brute force. On those, the agent can be graded against ground truth instead of against other agents.

The second is an independent anchor: a search engine written from scratch that shares no network and no code with the agent. It judges a move in the simplest way imaginable. From each candidate move it plays thousands of completely random games to the end, and it prefers the move whose random games win more often. That is crude. But its strength grows with its budget in a strikingly regular way — about +440 Elo for every tenfold increase in random games — which makes it a fixed external yardstick. Measured against that yardstick, the trained agents come out hundreds of Elo strong, in units a stranger could check for themselves.

Part 6 · The levers

Four things worked. A long list didn’t.

With the ruler calibrated, the experiments begin — and immediately deliver a humbling result. The project trained the small starter network four times, each time changing something that felt important: a fresh random start, feeding the solver’s perfect endgame answers into training, careful removal of duplicate positions from the training data. All four landed within ±3 points of one another. That is a plateau, and the ±3 rule had earned the right to call it one. Whatever was limiting the agent, it was none of those things.

Four changes eventually broke through. Two were found on the second day, in two-hour experiments. The other two took the third and fourth days, and each cost between six and nineteen hours of the GPU.

Lever one: a default nobody had questioned

Recall what the loop trains the network on. After each search, the search’s conclusions become the target that the network’s policy — its shortlist — is nudged toward. The formula that builds this target contains a dial. Call it by its code name, c_scale. It controls how strongly the target commits to what the search discovered, as opposed to staying close to the network’s own prior hunch. A widely used reference implementation ships with c_scale = 0.1: the search whispers. The original paper’s own text uses 1.0: the search speaks at full volume. This project inherited the whisper without noticing.

Lever two: a bigger brain

The other thing that worked is the least glamorous lever in machine learning: make the network bigger. So far the network has been a box with dials in it. To say what “bigger” means, the box has to be opened a little.

Two words from the figure carry the rest of this part. A filter is a small pattern detector, three cells by three, that is slid over the whole board and reports how strongly its pattern is present at every spot: “two of mine in a row with the third cell open”, say, or “an opponent stone next to a board that is already lost”. A layer holds many filters, and the number it holds is the network’s width: 64 in the starter net. A block is one round of “look at the neighbours, then update the picture”: two layers of filters in a row, plus a shortcut that lets the block’s input pass straight through and be added back at the end. The shortcut means a block only has to learn the correction to the picture it was handed, not the whole picture over again, and that is what lets many blocks stack without training going astray. The number of blocks is the network’s depth: six in the starter net. “Bigger”, then, means wider or deeper, and the two are different levers. This lever pulls width. The next pulls depth.

Width alone, going from 64 filters to 96, was worth +36 Elo. Width and the full-volume target from lever one together were worth +59. Widening again, to 128 filters, gave +77 Elo, the widest size tried. In dials that is half a million, then 1.1 million, then 1.9 million. The gains stack, though a little sub-additively: +36 and +40 combine to +59, not +76. That was where the second day ended, and where the first version of this page ended too. The next two levers needed the following two days, and whole nights of the GPU.

Lever three: a deeper brain

Width is one way to add dials. Depth is the other. A wider block notices more things at once. An extra block notices things about those things: a threat, then the send that the threat forces, then what that send costs. Every net on the ladder so far was six blocks deep. The experiment: keep the widest net, and stack eight blocks instead of six. That is 2.5 million dials.

It worked, narrowly. Head to head, the eight-block net beats the six-block net 53.3 % of the time, with the interval running from 50.5 to 56.0 %. In Elo that is +23, interval +4 to +42. The interval excludes zero, every secondary measure points the same way, and the 3.3-point margin just clears the rule from Part 5. It cost 1.36× the run time, which is almost exactly the extra arithmetic: the kitchen from Part 4 is now fully busy, so cost simply tracks work. Two more blocks, ten in all and 3.0 million dials, trained on the longer schedule described next, were worth another +35 over eight, interval +16 to +54. Neither step is dramatic. The first survived every check; the second did not survive the one that matters most, described in the fine print of Part 9: rerun from a second random start, the ten-block net beat the eight-block net by +9 instead of +35. And per point of Elo, depth turned out cheaper than width at this size — on the ladder’s instrument, which gives both sides the same number of simulations. Part 9 charges for the cost of each simulation as well, and the last of these steps does not survive the bill.

Lever four: keep going

Every run above stopped after 150 laps of the loop. Each lap moves 4,096 games forward by 64 moves apiece, which adds about a quarter of a million positions to a memory holding the most recent two million, and then trains the network on about as many positions drawn from that memory. The number 150 dated from when a run took twelve hours, and nobody had re-examined it once a run took two.

Training also follows a schedule. Each nudge to the dials has a size, called the learning rate. It starts large and is cut twice near the end of the run, so that the network can stop bouncing around and settle. In the 150-lap schedule the cuts came at laps 100 and 140. The experiment changed exactly one thing: take the eight-block recipe and run it for 300 laps, with the cuts at 200 and 280, so that each cut lands on a network that has seen twice as many games.

This was the single largest gain of the project. The 300-lap net beats its own 150-lap parent 67.5 % of the time, interval 65.0 to 70.0 %. That is +127 Elo, interval +108 to +147. Each learning-rate cut produced a visible step in the in-run curve. Against the reference, the 300-lap net stands at +211.

(Adding rungs on the ladder would predict +111 for that step, not +127. Elo is a dictionary for expected scores between pairs, not a conservation law. The two figures’ intervals overlap, and this much looseness is normal — worth knowing before you ever add two Elo gaps together.)

Why should that be? The honest answer is that the network was still learning. The 150-lap schedule had been ending runs before they had finished, and the evidence is an endgame statistic in Part 8 that moved twelve points with nothing else changed. The uncomfortable corollary: every comparison between architectures earlier on this page was made between networks that had not finished learning. When a 150-lap run said a net had “hit its ceiling”, some of the time it meant only that the net had stopped improving under that schedule. The ranking of the levers survived that correction. One belief about the game did not, and Part 8 tells that story in full.

Part 7 · The survival

Three faults, one lost run, and a lesson about saving your work

Two-hour experiments forgive almost anything. The long runs of the last two levers did not: six to nineteen hours each, overnight, unattended. And on the third night the hardware started fighting back. Three times, the GPU driver reported an illegal memory access and the process died on the spot. That error is the graphics card’s equivalent of a cook reaching for a shelf that isn’t there. All three faults happened in the same place. Not in self-play, which ran the pre-recorded order sequences of Part 4 for some sixty hours without incident. They struck in the periodic evaluation that a run performs on itself every few laps, which replays its own, differently shaped recordings at small batch sizes. Why that surface and not the other is still unproven. It could be a race inside the driver, or hardware that is marginal under exactly this load, and the response had to cover both.

The first suspect was churn. Each evaluation built four recording-and-replaying players from scratch and threw them away afterwards, and the third night was the first with four rather than three. Building them once per run and reusing them removed the churn entirely. As a bonus, it made every evaluation after the first about twice as fast. Then the third fault arrived anyway, on a run using the rebuilt code. That exonerated churn and left the evaluation recordings themselves as the suspect. The final, ten-block run therefore ran its evaluations without recordings at all. Zero faults in nineteen and a half hours, at a measured cost: evaluations six times slower, about three hours added to the run. In hindsight that was probably the worse trade, because the recovery machinery described next had already made a fault cost at most ten laps.

That machinery existed because the first two faults, forty minutes apart, had been expensive. They killed both runs queued that night, and one of them, the eight-block run, 79 laps in, could not be resumed. The reason is worth a picture. The network is small and was saved after every lap. The replay buffer of recent games is large and was saved only every tenth lap. Both went to the same file. So laps 71 through 78 had each overwritten the last save that contained any games, and the newest file on disk held a network with nothing to train on. Three hours of GPU time, gone, to a decision about file names.

With the two files in place, a wrapper script simply restarts a dead run in a fresh process, up to six times. The third fault, during the 300-lap run, was handled that way while nobody was watching: the run resumed from lap 99, lost at most ten laps, and finished. The same week added the other unglamorous machinery. Every run’s configuration is now recorded together with the exact code version that produced it, and a configuration refuses to be overwritten. That took thirty minutes on day three and should have existed on day one. The whole survival chapter is a case for doing the boring part first.

Part 8 · The game itself

What the agent believes about Ultimate Tic-Tac-Toe

This was the point of the whole exercise: turn the trained agent around and point it at the questions from Part 1. Every finding below was first measured on the early networks and then, on 2026-09-03, measured again on the strongest one (+242 Elo, 300 laps, ten blocks) with the verdict written down in advance: what result would count as the belief holding, and what would count as it having moved. Every ordering and every sign held. Two magnitudes moved, and the entries say so. Each finding gets the question every finding deserves: why should that be? One earlier finding did not survive at all. It gets its own section at the end of this part, because how it fell is the most instructive thing here. The full list of claims, with confidence intervals and the file each number came from, is the project’s KNOWLEDGE.md.

  • The folk wisdom is right: centre of the centre is the best first move. It came first in every single configuration tested, including the strongest network at 16,000 simulations, and the centre of an edge board came last in all of them. The ranking of all fifteen openings by the strongest net agrees with the earlier nets’ ranking almost perfectly (a rank correlation of 0.96), even though the strongest net rates X’s edge after the best move higher than the old ones did (+0.45 against +0.35 on the −1 to +1 scale). Report the ordering; distrust the magnitudes. Why should that be? Any centre cell sends your opponent into the centre board. The centre board is the one that sits on the most lines of boards: four, where a corner board touches three and an edge board only two. So every centre cell hands the opponent the prize board. Centre-of-centre is the only version of that gift where you took the prize board’s best cell for yourself first. Its evil twin explains the worst opening too. The centre of an edge board buys the best cell of the least connected board, and still hands your opponent the centre.
  • The openings are nearly flat — with two exceptions the stronger net found. After any decent first move, the top replies are almost equally good. The gap between them, on the network’s −1 to +1 scale where +1 is a certain win, is 0.03 or less in thirteen of the fifteen openings. You cannot win this game in the opening. You can only lose it slightly. The two exceptions are the openings whose best answer is to take the centre of the centre board yourself: there the strongest net sees a gap of 0.12 and 0.06 where the earlier nets saw 0.08 and 0.02. The only opening edges a stronger player has found are versions of “take the centre of the centre”. Why should that be? The send rule keeps handing the initiative back. Every strong move ships a choice to your opponent, so early advantages cannot be locked in, only banked in tiny increments. (That part is intuition. The ≤ 0.03 is measurement.)
  • Games are decided late — a marathon with a sprint finish. On games the agent had never seen, nothing is settled at move 30. The typical game is only called correctly from move 38 by the earlier nets, which is 78 % of the way through, and from move 36 by the two strongest nets — both of them, to the move, so the two-move gain with strength has already stopped. And the network’s glance-evaluation stays near the base rate until about move 28 (the timeline below shows the earlier nets’ numbers). Why should that be? The decisive currency of this game only becomes spendable at the end. Boards close in cascades, free moves and drawn boards crystallise, and the count comes into view. Until then almost everything is still negotiable.
  • A free move is worth about +0.2 — and half of that is the chance to end the game on the spot. A free move is a turn where the send rule does not apply and you may play anywhere. Its value was measured on the same −1 to +1 scale, by comparing thousands of real positions that are alike in every controllable respect except the free move: +0.16 ± 0.03 on the early nets, +0.20 ± 0.03 on both of the strongest (they agree to the third decimal). It is worth most late in the game, when level or ahead. Add one more thing to the comparison — whether the player can complete a line of three boards this very move — and the free move’s own worth drops to +0.08: much of a free move’s value is simply that it lets you cash a winning threat immediately instead of being sent somewhere else. Why should that be? A free move is the one moment you pick the battlefield instead of your opponent. But the measurement smuggles in a lovely statistics lesson. The naive correlation says free moves are bad, because they cluster in late positions that are already lost, the way hospital stays correlate with being ill. Only comparing like with like flips the sign and gives the honest +0.2. A cruder probe that edits the position by hand says +0.41 on the strongest net (+0.27 on the earlier ones); it overstates the effect by the same factor of two every time, and is read as an upper bound.
  • The “board hierarchy” is mostly line-counting in disguise. Centre board > corner board > edge board looks like deep positional truth. Control for the actual threats on the board of boards, and what carries value is each live line of boards: about ±0.15 per line, on the same scale, on every net. On the early nets owning a board was worth nothing beyond its lines. This is one of the two magnitudes that moved: on the strongest nets an owned board keeps a small value of its own, about +0.07, a fifth to a third of a line — and which class of board keeps the most is not resolved, because the two strongest nets order them differently. The network’s raw guess, asked directly, still puts centre above corner above edge by exactly the line count, and has done so unchanged through 340 Elo of improvement. Why should that be? A board has almost no intrinsic value. Only the lines through it can win the game. Centre > corner > edge is just 4 > 3 > 2 lines wearing a mystical costume, the same reason the centre square matters in ordinary tic-tac-toe. The small residual is probably the count rule: a board you own is a board the tiebreak counts, whatever its lines do.
  • X’s advantage is real — and stronger play means more draws, settled by counting. X, who moves first, takes 59–62 % of games between the strongest agents (57–60 % between the earlier ones), and 63 % of the strongest net’s own practice games. Raise both sides’ search budget and draws double, to 20 %; the strongest net playing itself at 256 simulations drew a quarter of the games. About a quarter of strong games are decided by the board count rather than by any line of boards (14 % by a count, 13 % by an equal count, which is a draw). And nearly every draw is the same shape: 97 % end four boards to four with exactly one board full and unwon, and in 63 % of them the lead in boards changed hands along the way. The strongest agents increasingly prove draws. Why should that be? Strong defence makes three boards in a row very hard to force. So the second scoring system inherits the verdict, like a knockout tournament where most finals go to penalties. The tiebreak rule is not a footnote to this game. At high level it is the game, a quarter of the time.
  • The commonest piece of human advice is wrong — as a rule. “Never send your opponent to a board they can win with one move” is the first thing every guide says. The strongest agent does exactly that in 24 % of its moves (a random legal move does it 36 % of the time), and the exact solver’s perfect move does it 69 % of the time in the endgame — 64 % even when the mover is winning. What is true is narrower: the perfect move never hands the opponent a line of three boards at once unless the game is already lost, and the agent almost never sends anyone to a winnable board in the opening (2 % of moves before move 20, 67 % after move 44). Why should that be? Late in the game most boards are nearly full, so most sends go to a board somebody can win, and a won local board only matters if it makes a line or moves the count. The advice is an opening principle and a line-of-boards principle dressed up as a law.

The belief that didn’t survive

The first version of this page carried one more entry in the list above. It is reproduced here exactly, because the point of what follows is how convincing it was.

  • as this page stated it on 2026-08-30 The intuition is draw-blind — and the search covers for it. Shown endgames that are provably drawn, the network’s value output recognises only about 55 % of them — at every network size tried. Yet in actual play with 256 simulations, the agent handles solved endgames 99.8 % perfectly. Why should that be? A draw is a proof, not a pattern: “neither side can achieve anything from here” means checking every idea and watching each one fail — which is precisely what lookahead does and a glance cannot. The division of labour between intuition and search, visible in a single statistic.

It is a good story. It has a mechanism. It matched a statistic that had held across six different recipes. It flattered the architecture of the whole project, and both outside reviewers accepted it. It was wrong in the part that mattered. The clue was hiding in the phrase “at every network size tried”: every size had been tried for the same 150 laps. Then lever four ran the eight-block recipe for 300 laps, with nothing else changed — same network, same labels, same loss. The value output’s draw recognition went from 55.6 % to 67.5 %. Two more blocks on the same schedule took it to 68.7 %.

Why should that be? This time the answer is offered as a guess, clearly labelled. A draw is the subtlest label a position can carry. A win and a loss each have a signature the network can pick up early: a line about to close, a count that cannot be caught. “Nothing works for either side” has no signature at all. It can only be learned from many drawn examples that look, on the surface, like everything else. That makes it the slowest thing in the game to learn, not an impossible one. This story fits the evidence better than the old one did. It is still a story. One more fact now sits under it, from looking at every saved snapshot of the two long runs: draw recognition does not climb slowly. It sits on a plateau for the first two hundred laps and then steps up, all at once, the moment the learning rate is cut — and so does almost every other measure of the network, from its endgame accuracy to how consistently it treats a board and its mirror image. The old nets were not too small. They were never annealed.

What the withdrawal teaches is more certain than any replacement, and it is the reason this section exists:

  • A why-story that fits is not evidence. The draw-is-a-proof story explained a real number with a real mechanism, and was accepted by everyone who read it. Nothing about a story’s elegance tells you whether the number it explains would move under a change nobody had tried.
  • Never diagnose what a network cannot learn from runs that were never trained to the end. “At every size tried” was true and meaningless. The thing that stayed constant was the schedule, not the networks. The honest phrasing of any such result is under this training budget.
  • “Search covers for intuition” is still real — but smaller than the page claimed. The best net’s raw shortlist picks the perfect endgame move 96.7 % of the time. With 256 simulations that becomes 99.9 %. The average regret, meaning the value given away compared with the perfect move on the −1 to +1 scale, is 0.001. The search still closes a gap. It is three points wide, not the chasm the old entry implied.
  • The earlier “capacity” conclusions were really conclusions about training. That is why Part 6 now says the small net’s four-run plateau held under that schedule, and why Part 9 has to ask one of its questions a second time.

Part 9 · The standing

“Strongest” is a claim about a budget

So the deepest network on the longest schedule is the best agent. It stands +242 Elo over the reference, and its raw shortlist alone picks the perfect move in 96.7 % of solved endgames. Time to declare victory? Two independent outside reviews of the project forced a sharper question, back on the second day when the best agent was only +77. (One review filed sixteen findings; each was answered in writing.) The question: best under what conditions? Every ladder comparison gives each agent 64 simulations per move. But the wide network of that day cost about four times the arithmetic per simulation of the small reference net, because a block’s cost grows with the square of its width and 128² is four times 64². So here is the fair fight: give the small net four times the simulations, so that both sides spend the same total arithmetic per move. Who wins?

The rest of the honest fine print, straight from the review and still true:

  • Only the ends of the width trend are settled. Smallest to widest is worth +5.1 points, with the interval running from +1.2 to +9.1. The individual steps in between sit inside the noise band.
  • The top of the ladder has now been repeated from a second random start — and its last rung did not survive. On the small net, repeating a recipe from a different random start moves the result by up to ±2.5 points. On 2026-09-05 the ten-block, 300-lap recipe was rerun from a second random start: +213 Elo over the reference against the first seed’s +242, a dead heat between the two seeds head to head (+4, interval −13 to +22), and +9 over the eight-block net where the first seed had measured +35. So the band at the top of the ladder is about 3 points; the +127 duration step dwarfs it, and the +35 depth step sits inside it. The ladder’s shape is duration, not depth, and its small steps are not individually settled.
  • The endgame set was no longer a clean test — and the confirmation has since been paid. It had been consulted so often while the recipes were being tuned that it counted as a development set, not a held-out one. On 2026-09-03 a fresh set was built from the strongest play, split into a development half and a test half by source game before any position was solved; on the development half the strongest net scores 84.7 %, within a tenth of a point of its 84.6 % on the old set. The test half was read once, on 2026-09-05, after the last training run: 85.0 %, with the two other strong nets within three tenths of a point of it.
  • A suspected bias in the test suite was hunted down and bounded. The worry was that the suite’s board orientations might favour one side. The effect was measured at 0.6 points or less: a real concern, of negligible size.

The fair fight, asked again

Two days and two levers later, the question deserved a second asking. It produced the last measurements on this page: three ten-minute matches run on the morning of the pause. Per simulation, the ten-block net costs about 6.7 times the small reference net. The cost grows with the number of blocks and with the square of the width, since each of 128 features is computed from all 128 inputs of the previous block, so the ratio is 10 × 128² against 6 × 64². The fair fight is therefore the small net at 427 simulations against the deep net at 64. This time the deep net wins. It scores 56.0 %, interval 53.2 to 58.6 %, which is +42 Elo, interval +23 to +61. The second day’s story, that the small net which thinks longer beats the big one, was true of six-block nets at 150 laps. It does not survive depth and duration.

Why should that be? Search amplifies what the network’s glance provides. It cannot manufacture what the glance lacks. Four hundred simulations is a long look, but a bounded one, and a search that trusts wrong verdicts at the ends of its lines multiplies them rather than fixing them. The small net, remember, misjudged nearly half of all drawn endgames. Give the search a net that knows the endgame, and 64 simulations goes further than 427 without. That is a mechanism that fits. After Part 8, hold it as loosely as the last one.

The second match is the other half of the lesson, and it is a null. At equal compute, the last rung of the ladder disappears. Ten blocks cost 1.25 times eight, so the match is the ten-block net at 64 simulations against the eight-block 300-lap net at 80. The ten-block net scores 48.4 %, interval 45.7 to 51.1 %, which is −11 Elo, interval −30 to +7: inside the noise band. The +35 that depth bought at equal simulations is exactly what its extra cost per simulation buys back as search — and the second random start, run after the pause, found most of that +35 was never there to begin with. Duration is different. Going from 150 to 300 laps costs nothing extra per move, and was worth +127. So “neither depth nor duration is exhausted” needs its own qualifier. On the ladder’s instrument, both still pay. At a fixed thinking budget, only duration has shown that it does.

Spending the search where the game is decided

One more play-time idea came from the timeline in Part 8. If nothing is settled before move 30 and the typical game is decided at move 38, why give every move the same number of simulations? The schedule tested gives the first 24 moves 128 simulations each, and every move after that 384. Over a typical fifty-move game that is about the same total as a flat 256 per move. On the small reference net the schedule was worth +50 Elo. Part of that, it turned out, was simply extra simulations in games that ran long. On the eight-block 300-lap net it scored 53.7 %, interval 51.2 to 56.2 %, which is +26 Elo, interval +8 to +43: a real, modest gain at no extra cost. On the best net, in the third match of the pause morning, it scored 51.4 %, interval 48.9 to 53.7 %, which is +10 Elo, interval −7 to +26. That is below the rule. The gain shrinks as the net grows stronger, and on the strongest one it is not a claim, so the agent plays a flat 256. Why should that be? A guess: at 256 simulations the best net already plays solved endgames 99.9 % perfectly, so the extra late simulations have little left to buy, while the thinner early search still costs something.

And then the loop stopped turning — by decision, not by accident. Four days: two spent building and calibrating, two scaling under review. Fifteen completed training runs. A ladder whose last four rungs climbed from +77 to +242 along two directions, depth and duration, and neither has been shown to be exhausted on the ladder’s own terms. The obvious next experiments write themselves: twelve blocks, or 600 laps. Each is a committed GPU-day, which is exactly why the project paused at this fork instead of sleepwalking through it.

One branch keeps climbing. The morning’s equal-compute matches say that if it does, the run to buy is the longer one, not the deeper one. The other branch turns the agent around: redraw the opening atlas with a net 240 points stronger than the one that drew it; re-run every belief in Part 8 on the strongest net, with a written note beforehand of what “moved” would look like; and find out, from the checkpoints already on disk, what the value output learned about draws between lap 150 and lap 300. The plan written at the pause leaned toward the second branch, on the grounds that Part 8’s withdrawn belief was exactly what building higher on unverified beliefs looks like — and the day after the pause, that branch was taken. Part 8 now carries the re-run: every ordering and sign held, two magnitudes moved, and the checkpoints answered the draw question with a step at the first learning-rate cut. The stronger net also gave the pause one more argument for staying paused: on every belief, the eight-block and ten-block nets agree with each other to within their error bars, so the last rung of the ladder changed nothing about what the agent knows — and a second ten-block seed, trained after the pause as the control, reproduces every one of those beliefs and every layer-by-layer reading of Part 8 to within a few points, while cutting the last rung itself down to the noise. Whatever comes next will be judged the way everything here was: on the frozen paired suite, against the anchors, believing nothing below +3.

That discipline is the actual result of the project. The strong player is nice, and will be replaced the next time a GPU-day is spent. Knowing which beliefs about an unsolved game deserve belief, exactly how much, and how one of them was withdrawn the day the evidence moved: that is the part that generalises to everything else you will ever measure.
