# Exact / Near-Exact Solving Feasibility for Ultimate Tic-Tac-Toe

**Question:** instead of (or alongside) training an AlphaZero-style net, can we just *solve* the user's variant — closed local boards, free move on redirect-to-closed, 3-in-a-row-of-boards wins, otherwise most local boards won wins, exact tie in board count draws?

**Short answer:** No published work solves this variant, or even the plain closed-board variant. The one existing "solved" result (Bertholon et al. 2020) is a **pure hand-written combinatorial proof, not a computer search**, and it solves a *different* rule set (open boards). The state space of the closed-board game is roughly **10^33–10^38** positions — five to ten orders of magnitude beyond Othello, the largest game ever weakly solved (~10^28 states). A full retrograde solve is impossible on any existing hardware. What *is* buildable on this machine is a small, exact **endgame tablebase** for late-game positions (see §5), which is a genuinely useful complement to the neural agent rather than a substitute for it.

## 1. Bertholon et al. 2020 — what was actually solved, and how

[Bertholon, Géraud-Stewart, Kugelmann, Lenoir & Naccache, "At Most 43 Moves, At Least 29: Optimal Strategies and Bounds for Ultimate Tic-Tac-Toe," arXiv:2006.02353](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.02353) (ENS Paris, June 2020).

**Rule variant solved (verified from the full text, not just the abstract):** their U3T is the **open-board** variant. Quoting the paper directly: *"When a player aligns three of their marks... we say they win that field. Any further action in that field will not change this status and it is not possible to win an already won field."* Figure 2's caption makes this explicit: a player can still legally place a mark in an already-won field — "such a move would therefore have no effect" — because redirection only becomes free-choice when the target field has **no free spots** (i.e., is *full*), not merely when it is *won*. This is **not** the user's closed-board variant, where a won board is immediately dead to further play. Their game also ends in a plain **draw** if the board fills with no 3-line of fields — there is no "most boards" tiebreak.

**Method:** this is the paper's most surprising feature relative to the task's assumption — **there is no computer search anywhere in it**. No alpha-beta, no retrograde analysis, no memoization, no state-space enumeration, no reported CPU-hours or memory. It is a classical constructive combinatorial-game-theory proof, in the tradition of pairing strategies:
- An explicit three-phase strategy for the first player ("Xavier"): an **opening** (play the center of the center field, forcing 8 replies into the center field), a **middlegame** (a forcing sequence that wins an entire field in ≤17 extra moves via a two-field "ping-pong" redirect trick), and an **endgame** (a simple positional rule — play spot 0 of the active field if open, else spot 8 — proven to always eventually complete a winning line).
- Six invariant properties (P1–P6) are proved by induction to hold move-by-move throughout the middlegame/endgame, which is what makes the strategy rigorously *guaranteed* rather than merely empirically strong.
- The bounds are proved, not measured: the 43-move upper bound comes from bounding the strategy's own move count; the 29-move lower bound comes from an explicit resistance strategy for the second player ("Olivia," the "LBS" — Least-Blocking-Spot heuristic) proved to force at least 9 rounds before any field has 2 marks of the same color, hence ≥6 more Xavier moves are needed after that to complete 3 fields.
- **First-move result:** by an adversarial pairing argument (if Xavier's first move isn't on the main diagonal — a "double" (i,i) — Olivia can trap him in a 2-field stalemate for 46 turns, contradicting the 43-move bound), the paper proves the optimal first move must be a double, and (Lemma 7) pins down the optimal second move given any Olivia reply.

**Result:** first player ("X") has a **forced win** under these (open-board, plain-draw) rules; optimal play takes between 29 and 43 total moves.

**Compute used:** **none reported — effectively zero**, since the result is proved by hand, not searched. This directly contradicts the premise that solving requires large compute; it only does when the proof can't be made constructively, which is exactly the case for the closed-board / most-boards variant (§5).

**Follow-ups found:** [Diamond 2022, "A Practical Method for Preventing Forced Wins in Ultimate Tic-Tac-Toe," arXiv:2207.06239](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06239) — uses the *same* open-board rules and proposes randomizing the first 4 moves (5 random digits 0–8) to dodge the forced-win line, computing the collision probability as 56/59,049 ≈ 0.095%. It does not touch the closed-board variant, run any code, or add computation. **No GitHub repository from the Bertholon et al. authors was found** — the paper cites only a pre-2013 third-party implementation as prior art, not their own code. Bertholon et al. also cite [George & Janoski 2016, "Group Actions on Winning Games of Super Tic-Tac-Toe," arXiv:1606.04779](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04779), which formalizes the board's symmetry as a **dihedral group action** (order 8) partitioning winning configurations into equivalence classes — the group-theoretic basis for the symmetry reduction used below. **No source found states or analyzes the closed-board variant's game value** — it is an open question in the literature.

## 2. State-space size for the closed-board variant

No paper publishes a reachable-position count for closed-board UTTT (confirmed by exhausting the obvious searches; even [Nelson Elhage's dedicated solver project](https://minimax.dev/docs/ultimate/pruning/) writes "I have not yet attempted to perform the exact combinatorics.., I currently do not see a clear way to build endgame databases"). Below is an original back-of-envelope estimate.

**Naive upper bound.** Each of 81 spots is X/O/empty: 3^81. Since log₁₀3 ≈ 0.47712, 81×0.47712 ≈ 38.65, so **3^81 ≈ 4.4 × 10^38**.

**Per-local-board reduction.** For a *standalone* game of tic-tac-toe that stops the instant someone gets 3-in-a-row, the count of legally-reachable board patterns is **5,478** (down from the raw 3^9 = 19,683), and only **765** up to the 8-fold symmetry of a single board ([Wikipedia, Game complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_complexity); [Friedrich, "The state space of Tic-Tac-Toe is much smaller than 9!"](https://medium.com/@carsten.friedrich/the-state-space-of-tic-tac-toe-is-actually-much-smaller-than-9-43688c0819a4)). The classic 5,478 figure assumes marks in *that* board alternate X,O,X,O,…. In UTTT this assumption **breaks**: the redirect rule lets a player be sent back into the same local board repeatedly while the opponent's move goes into a different board, so a local board's fill order need not alternate. A local board's true reachable-state count for the closed variant is therefore somewhere in **[5,478, 19,683]**, and no exact figure is published.

Using these two ends as brackets across the 9 boards (independently, ignoring inter-board legality constraints, as a first approximation):
- Lower bracket: 4.4×10^38 × (5,478/19,683)^9 ≈ 4.4×10^38 × (0.2783)^9 ≈ **4×10^33**
- Upper bracket: unreduced, **≈4×10^38**

So the closed-board UTTT state space is plausibly **10^33–10^38 reachable positions**, before the further (modest) reduction from the 8-fold dihedral board symmetry (worked out below) or macro-level legality constraints (e.g., the macro-board can't already show two different players' 3-lines).

**Symmetry reduction, computed via Burnside's lemma.** The board's symmetry group is D₄ (order 8: 4 rotations + 4 reflections), acting *identically* on the macro-grid and every local grid at once. For a bare 3×3 grid, the fixed-cell counts under the 8 group elements are 9 (identity), 1, 1, 1 (three rotations), 3, 3, 3, 3 (four reflections) — sum 24, giving 24/8 = **3 orbits** (corner/edge/center), the standard tic-tac-toe symmetry result. For the 81 opening cells (macro-cell, local-cell) pairs — since a symmetry must fix both coordinates simultaneously — Burnside gives Σf(g)² / 8 = (9²+1²+1²+1²+3²+3²+3²+3²)/8 = (81+3+36)/8 = 120/8 = **15 distinct first-move equivalence classes** out of 81 raw choices. This ~5.4× reduction applies (at least) to opening-move analysis; a comparable ~8× reduction applies to full-position counts generically, though far too small to change the 10^33–10^38 order of magnitude.

**Comparison with actually-solved games:**

| Game | State-space size | Solved how | Compute |
|---|---|---|---|
| Connect Four | 4,531,985,219,092 (≈4.5×10^12) reachable positions | Allis 1988 (knowledge-based search); Tromp built a full strong-solution lookup database via exhaustive 8-ply-forward enumeration + symmetry/subgame compression | Fits on a single modern disk; recomputed on a laptop today ([Tromp](https://tromp.github.io/c4/c4.html), [play4row](https://play4row.com/connect-four-solver)) |
| Checkers | ≈5×10^20 total | Schaeffer et al. 2007 — proof-number/alpha-beta forward search + a **10-piece-or-fewer retrograde endgame database (39 trillion, ≈3.9×10^13, positions)** | ~18 years, hundreds of CPUs cumulative ([Schaeffer et al., *Science* 2007](https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~jonathan/publications/ai_publications/checksolved.pdf); [historyofinformation.com](https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=2476)) |
| Othello (8×8) | ≈10^28 total positions | Takizawa 2023 — weakly solved (draw); forward alpha-beta from the empty board **backed by an exact database at 36-empty-squares (1,505,367,525 positions), itself built from a 50-empty-squares database (2,958,551 positions)**; ≈1.5×10^18 positions examined in total | Preferred Networks' MN-J supercomputer (mixed Xeon/EPYC nodes) ([arXiv:2310.19387](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19387)) |
| **UTTT, closed-board (this project)** | **~10^33 – 10^38 reachable positions** (estimate above) | **Unsolved** | N/A |

Even Othello's 10^28-state space — the largest ever weakly solved, using a national-lab-class supercomputer over an extended project — is **5 to 10 orders of magnitude smaller** than the closed-board UTTT estimate. A full retrograde solve (build a lookup table of every position's value, à la Connect Four) is not a matter of "more time on this machine" — at even 1 bit per position, 10^33 positions require ~1.25×10^32 bytes, versus this machine's 128 GB (1.37×10^11 bytes) RAM and 290 GB disk: **short by roughly 21 orders of magnitude.** This rules out full retrograde solving categorically, on this or any currently conceivable machine.

## 3. Search-based (non-retrograde) approaches

**Alpha-beta / bitboards in practice.** CodinGame's "Ultimate Tic-Tac-Toe" bot-programming puzzle is the main venue where strong engines have been built and benchmarked under real time limits (~100ms/move):
- [nelhage/ultimattt](https://github.com/nelhage/ultimattt) ([writeup](https://blog.nelhage.com/post/solving-ultimate-ttt/), [docs](https://minimax.dev/docs/ultimate/)) is the most serious *solving* attempt found. Rust engine, 40-byte immutable bitboard positions, combined **two-level Proof Number Search + Depth-First PNS (df-pn)**, a parallel transposition table, and a positional-analysis pruning layer (statically detecting "dead" moves that hand the opponent a forced win). Reported result: solves positions from **~20 ply (10 moves each) in a few hours on a Ryzen 3900X** — notably the *same CPU model* as this project's machine. The author's own extrapolation: **"a few hundred million CPU-hours to solve the whole game," costing an estimated $2M–$10M on AWS**. That is ~3×10^8 CPU-hours; even fully saturating a 3900X's 24 threads nonstop for a year is only ~2.1×10^5 core-hours, so this machine alone would need **1,000+ years** to replicate that estimate for the *easier* open-board rules — and the user's variant is not obviously easier.
- [ai-ultimate-tictactoe (Rust crate)](https://docs.rs/ai-ultimate-tictactoe/latest/ai_ultimate_tictactoe/) implements the standard **closed-board** rule ("a claimed board closes immediately... if the destination board is closed, the opponent may use any empty cell in any open board") with iterative-deepening alpha-beta, PVS, a transposition table, and a precomputed tactics table over all 19,683 raw per-board patterns — a strong practical player, not a solver.
- Hobbyist/course submissions (Illinois CS440, Columbia UTTTSolver, various GitHub minimax bots) report going **useless past depth ~4–6** with plain alpha-beta, and mostly fall back to MCTS; CodinGame Legend-league bots (e.g., reCurse's AlphaZero-style "Totozero," [forum writeup](https://forum.codingame.com/t/spring-challenge-2021-feedbacks-strategies/190849/67)) report thousands to tens of thousands of playouts per 100ms move, not exhaustive search.
- **No claim of "solved from move N onward" for UTTT (any variant) was found anywhere** — unlike Othello's "solved from 36 empty" milestone, no comparable partial-solve milestone exists in public sources.

**Proof-number search and the "most boards" tiebreak.** Standard PNS/df-pn assume a **binary** win/loss objective at internal nodes (a position is proven, disproven, or unknown); draws are typically handled by running the search twice (once treating a draw as a loss, once as a win) — see the [Proof-Number Search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof-number_search) literature. The user's variant is worse than a simple draw case: when all 9 local boards close **without** a 3-in-a-row, the outcome depends on a **count comparison** (who has more boards), which is a *scored*, not boolean, terminal condition — closer to a Node in a general-sum evaluation than a provable win/loss leaf. The applicable machinery is **[Multiple-Outcome Proof Number Search (MOPNS), Saffidine & Cazenave 2012](https://www.lamsade.dauphine.fr/~cazenave/papers/mopns.pdf)**, which generalizes PNS to games with more than two outcomes without re-running the whole search per outcome, and **[Kishimoto & Müller's two-phase df-pn](https://www.chessprogramming.org/Proof-Number_Search)**, which reuses the transposition table across the "is it a win" / "is it at least a draw" passes. Adapting either to a *count-based* tiebreak (rather than a discrete ranked outcome set) is not off-the-shelf — it is a genuine, non-trivial extension the user's variant would require, separate from the sheer size problem.

**Endgame databases keyed on (macro-state, per-open-board-state).** This is the practically important idea, worked out with real numbers in §5.

No project — academic or hobbyist — has produced a first-move value **table** with proven (as opposed to simulated) values, for any UTTT variant. The closest thing to ground truth anywhere is Bertholon's proof that, under open-board rules, the first player wins and the optimal opening is forced to be a "double."

## 5. Recommendation: what's realistically buildable here

**Not feasible on this machine (or any known machine):** a full retrograde solve of the closed-board (or closed-board + most-boards) variant. The estimated 10^33–10^38-position space exceeds the 128 GB RAM / 290 GB disk budget by ~20+ orders of magnitude, and exceeds even Othello's supercomputer-scale weak solve by 5–10 orders of magnitude. A rigorous, from-the-empty-board proof of the game's value (à la Bertholon, but for this harder variant) is also unlikely without either a new hand-constructed pairing-strategy proof (unknown whether one exists for these rules) or a PNS/df-pn campaign plausibly needing "hundreds of millions of CPU-hours" — likely **years of this machine's full-time compute** — before even solving the MOPNS-style multi-outcome-search problem the tiebreak introduces.

**Feasible and worth building: an exact endgame tablebase keyed on "K local boards still open."** Model a position as (which 9−K boards are closed, with value X/O/Draw ∈ {3 symbols}) × (full internal state of each of the K open boards, ≤19,683 raw patterns each) × (whose move / which board is targeted, ~10-way). Sizing:

- **K=1 open board:** 9 (choice of open board) × 3^8 (closed-board values) × 19,683 (open board's states) × ~10 (turn info) ≈ **1.16×10^10 positions.** At 1 byte/position that's **~11.6 GB** — comfortably fits in 128 GB RAM even generously encoded (8 bytes/entry ≈ 93 GB still fits).
- **K=2 open boards:** 36 × 3^7 × 19,683² × ~10 ≈ **3.05×10^14 positions** — even at 1 bit/position that is ~38 TB, ~130× over the 290 GB disk budget. Not feasible as an exhaustive combinatorial enumeration; only reachable-position generation (via actual forward game-tree exploration rather than the full cross-product) plus heavy compression and symmetry (÷8) could conceivably bring this within reach as a stretch goal, but it is not a safe bet.
- **K≥3:** combinatorially far worse (K=3 ≈ 4.7×10^18) — clearly out of reach.

So the concrete, defensible target is: **an exact tablebase for "≤1 local board still open"** (comfortably fits in RAM), with K=2 as an ambitious stretch using reachable-only generation rather than full enumeration. Build it in the user's stack (Numba on the 3900X's 12 cores/24 threads is well suited to this — it's dense bitboard enumeration and win-checking, not branchy tree search) via straightforward backward induction from terminal states, since the state graph is a DAG once boards start closing (a closed board can never reopen).

**How this complements the AlphaZero agent, concretely:**
- **Ground-truth verification of the value head.** In the last 1–2 open boards, the tablebase gives the *exact* game-theoretic value (accounting correctly for the most-boards tiebreak). Comparing the trained net's value output against this ground truth at every such position encountered in test games gives a precise, provable error rate for the network's endgame judgment — something self-play win-rate alone cannot give.
- **Perfect endgame play / hybrid engine.** Splicing the tablebase into MCTS as a terminal lookup (exactly how Schaeffer's checkers proof and Takizawa's Othello proof used their databases as leaves for forward search) gives provably perfect play in the last stretch of any game, strictly upgrading the trained agent's practical strength for free.
- **Opening-move statistics, not proofs.** The 15-class first-move symmetry reduction (§2) means only 15 openings need distinguishing. A long, PNS/df-pn-assisted or heavily-simulated search over those 15 (mirroring nelhage's approach, budgeted for weeks rather than years) could plausibly rank or bound several of them with high confidence, but a *proof* akin to Bertholon's "must be a double" result for this variant should be treated as a research-level reach goal, not a deliverable.
- **What needs a cluster:** anything beyond K≈1–2 boards open, any claim of the form "solved from ply N" analogous to Othello's milestone, and definitely a full-game proof. These would need Othello-scale (national-lab supercomputer, sustained multi-month project) resources at minimum, and given the state-space gap, quite possibly more.

## Key takeaways

- Bertholon et al. 2020 (arXiv:2006.02353) is a **hand-written proof, zero compute** — not a computer search — and it solves the **open-board** variant (won boards stay playable "with no effect" until full) with a plain draw, not the user's closed-board + most-boards variant; no code from the authors exists.
- Under Bertholon's rules, X forces a win in 29–43 moves; the optimal first move is proven to be one of the 9 diagonal "doubles."
- The closed-board variant's game value is an **open question** in the literature — no paper states or attempts it.
- The closed-board UTTT state space is estimated at **~10^33–10^38 reachable positions** (own back-of-envelope, bracketed from 3^81 ≈ 4.4×10^38 down via the 5,478–19,683 per-board reachability range); no authoritative published count exists.
- This dwarfs every solved game: Connect Four (~4.5×10^12, solved), Checkers (~5×10^20 total, weakly solved via a 3.9×10^13-position endgame DB), Othello (~10^28 total, weakly solved in 2023 via ~1.5×10^18 positions searched on a supercomputer).
- A full retrograde solve is impossible here by roughly **20+ orders of magnitude** versus the 128 GB RAM / 290 GB disk budget.
- The best public solving attempt, [nelhage/ultimattt](https://minimax.dev/docs/ultimate/) (PNS+df-pn on a Ryzen 3900X — this project's exact CPU), solves ~20-ply endgames in hours but estimates **hundreds of millions of CPU-hours** to finish the (easier, open-board) game.
- The "most boards" tiebreak turns terminal evaluation into a scored/multi-outcome problem, requiring Multiple-Outcome PNS-style adaptations rather than off-the-shelf binary PNS/df-pn.
- The board's 8-fold dihedral symmetry provably collapses the 81 first moves into **15 equivalence classes** (Burnside's lemma, derived above).
- Realistic, buildable target: an **exact "≤1 open local board" endgame tablebase** (~1.16×10^10 positions, ~12 GB) — trivially fits in RAM; a "≤2 open boards" version (~3×10^14 combinatorial upper bound) is a stretch goal only via reachable-only generation, not full enumeration.
- Use the tablebase as ground truth to measure the AlphaZero net's endgame value-head error and as a perfect-play terminal lookup spliced into its search — not as a path to solving the full game.
