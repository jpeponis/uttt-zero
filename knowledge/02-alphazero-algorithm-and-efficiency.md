# 02 — The AlphaZero algorithm family and what makes it work at small scale

Scope: what the published algorithms actually specify (numbers, not folklore), which later improvements matter on one home PC (RTX 3090 24 GB + RTX 3060 12 GB, Ryzen 9 3900X, 128 GB RAM, Windows 11, PyTorch, no C++ compiler, no JAX-GPU), and how all of it maps onto Ultimate Tic-Tac-Toe (UTTT: 81 cells, ≤81 plies, branching mostly ≤9, occasionally 81 when sent to a closed board). Companion files: `04-compute-and-engineering.md` (hardware/throughput), `05-strategy-probing-and-interpretability.md`.

## 1. The algorithm as published

### 1.1 AlphaGo Zero (Silver et al., Nature 2017)

Source: [Nature 550:354](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature24270); open-access preprint with the full Methods: [UCL Discovery PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10045895/1/agz_unformatted_nature.pdf).

- **Network.** Input 19×19×17 (8 planes own stones history, 8 opponent, 1 colour-to-play). Trunk: one conv block (256 filters 3×3, BN, ReLU) then 19 or 39 residual blocks (each: conv 256 3×3 → BN → ReLU → conv 256 3×3 → BN → skip → ReLU). Policy head: 1×1 conv with 2 filters → BN → ReLU → FC to 19²+1 = 362 logits. Value head: 1×1 conv with 1 filter → BN → ReLU → FC 256 → ReLU → FC 1 → tanh. Ablation (Fig. 4): residual vs plain conv ≈ +600 Elo; a dual-headed net vs separate policy/value nets ≈ another +600 Elo, attributed to the shared representation regularising both heads.
- **Search (PUCT).** Edge statistics {N, W, Q, P}; select `argmax_a Q(s,a) + U(s,a)` with `U = c_puct · P(s,a) · sqrt(Σ_b N(s,b)) / (1 + N(s,a))`. `c_puct` itself was tuned by Gaussian-process optimisation and never published. Leaf nodes always expanded; children initialised to N=W=Q=0. Leaves are evaluated under a **random dihedral transformation** (one of 8) each time, and positions are queued for the net in **mini-batches of 8** with the search thread blocked until evaluation returns; **virtual loss** keeps parallel threads on different paths. Subtree reuse between moves. 1,600 simulations/move (~0.4 s on 4 TPUs).
- **Exploration.** Root prior `P = (1−ε) p + ε η`, `η ~ Dir(α)`, `α = 0.03`, `ε = 0.25`. Move selection ∝ `N^(1/τ)` with `τ = 1` for the first 30 moves, then `τ → 0` (greedy).
- **Training targets and loss.** `(z − v)² − πᵀ log p + c‖θ‖²`, c = 1e-4, policy target = root visit distribution, value target = game outcome from the mover's perspective. Cross-entropy and MSE weighted equally *for RL*; in the supervised comparison the MSE term was down-weighted by 0.01 "to avoid overfitting to the values".
- **Optimisation.** SGD momentum 0.9, mini-batch 2,048 (64 GPU workers × 32), sampled uniformly from **all positions of the most recent 500,000 games**; a checkpoint every 1,000 steps; LR schedule 10⁻² → 10⁻³ → 10⁻⁴ (Extended Data Table 3; ELF OpenGo reproduces it as a three-stage 10⁻²/10⁻³/10⁻⁴ schedule, see [alphaXiv summary](https://www.alphaxiv.org/overview/1902.04522)). Each iteration the best player generates **25,000 games**.
- **Evaluation gating.** Each checkpoint plays **400 games** (1,600 sims, τ→0) against the incumbent and replaces it only if it wins **> 55 %**.
- **Resignation.** Threshold set automatically to keep false positives < 5 %; 10 % of games are played out without resignation to measure that.
- **Symmetries.** Data augmentation with all rotations/reflections during training, plus the random transform at search time (Methods, "Domain knowledge" item 4).
- **Scale.** 20-block/3-day run: 4.9 M games, 700k mini-batches. 40-block/40-day run: 29 M games, 3.1 M mini-batches; final Elo 5,185 with search vs **3,055 for the raw policy network** — search adds ~2,000 Elo even at the end.

### 1.2 AlphaZero (Silver et al., Science 2018)

Sources: [arXiv 1712.01815](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01815); [Science 362:1140](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aar6404). Constants from the published pseudocode are mirrored as defaults in DeepMind's [mctx `muzero_policy`](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mctx/blob/main/mctx/_src/policies.py).

- **No gating.** "Self-play games are generated by using the latest parameters for this neural network, omitting the evaluation step and the selection of best player." The same hyperparameters for all three games; the only game-specific knob is the noise.
- **PUCT with a growing exploration constant:** `U = P · sqrt(N_parent)/(1+N_child) · (c_init + log((N_parent + c_base + 1)/c_base))`, `c_init = 1.25`, `c_base = 19,652` (pseudocode; mctx defaults `pb_c_init=1.25`, `pb_c_base=19652`).
- **Dirichlet noise scaled to the branching factor:** `α = 0.3` chess (~35 legal moves), `0.15` shogi (~92), `0.03` Go (~250); `ε = 0.25`. The Oracle Connect-Four team extrapolated this as the rule of thumb **`α ≈ 10 / (average number of legal moves)`** ([Prasad, Lessons from AlphaZero part 3](https://medium.com/oracledevs/lessons-from-alphazero-part-3-parameter-tweaking-4dceb78ed1e5)); the AlphaZero.jl docs state the same heuristic ([params reference](https://jonathan-laurent.github.io/AlphaZero.jl/dev/reference/params/)). KataGo generalises it as `α = 0.03 · 19² / (number of legal moves)` ([Wu 2019 §2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)). For UTTT (≈7 legal moves on a typical constrained turn) this gives **α ≈ 1–1.5**; Oracle settled on α = 1.0 for Connect Four (≈4 legal moves) after trying 2.5 and 1.75.
- **Temperature:** moves sampled ∝ visit count for the first 30 moves (`num_sampling_moves = 30` in the pseudocode), then greedy; evaluation is always greedy.
- **800 simulations/move**; 40/80/200 ms per move in chess/shogi/Go on 4 TPUs.
- **Training:** 700k steps of mini-batch 4,096; LR 0.2 dropped to 0.02, 0.002, 0.0002 (at 100k/300k/500k steps in the pseudocode); momentum 0.9; weight decay 1e-4; replay window 1,000,000 games; checkpoint every 1,000 steps. 5,000 first-generation TPUs for self-play, 16 second-generation TPUs for training. Games: 44 M chess (9 h), 24 M shogi (12 h), 21 M Go (34 h) (Table S3).
- **Batching/virtual loss** as in AlphaGo Zero; the leela-zero maintainers' reading of the pseudocode (window 1e6, checkpoint every 1,000 steps, batch 4,096) is in [leela-zero #2416](https://github.com/leela-zero/leela-zero/issues/2416).

### 1.3 Implementation details the papers leave implicit (and what others use)

- **Unvisited children.** AlphaGo Zero initialises Q = 0, which on a [−1, 1] scale is "draw" and makes unexplored moves look attractive to a losing side. KataGo uses **first-play urgency**: `V(c) = V(parent) − 0.2·sqrt(P_explored)` (0 at a noised root). MiniZero initialises unvisited children to `Q_Σ/(N_Σ+1)` (the mean of visited siblings with one virtual loss) and reports a significant gain in 9×9 Go but not in Othello ([MiniZero §III-B, Fig. 8](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.11305v3)).
- **c_puct in practice.** AlphaZero 1.25 (growing with log N); KataGo 1.1; AlphaZero.jl Connect Four 2.0; Oracle Connect Four ≈ 4 (they observed 30–50 % duplicated positions in the window at c = 1, less at c = 4); AlphaZero_Gomoku 5. Larger boards/priors → smaller c; small nets and short games tolerate larger c.
- **Policy softmax temperature 1.03 at the root** (KataGo, from SAI) as a restoring force against premature policy collapse ([KataGoMethods.md](https://github.com/lightvector/KataGo/blob/master/docs/KataGoMethods.md)).

## 2. Efficiency improvements that matter at hobby scale

### 2.1 KataGo (Wu 2019, [arXiv 1902.10565](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565))

Headline: surpasses ELF OpenGo's final strength after **19 days on ≤ 28 V100s (~1.4 GPU-years) vs ELF's ~74 GPU-years — ≈50× less compute**, and ≈10× less than Leela Zero; "using our code, it is possible to reach strong or top human amateur strength starting from nothing on even single GPUs in mere days". Ablation table (Elo after ≈2 days / factor more compute needed without the technique): playout-cap randomisation 1.37×, forced playouts + policy target pruning 1.25×, global pooling 1.60×, auxiliary opponent-policy target 1.30×, ownership + score targets 1.65×, Go-specific inputs 1.55×; product ≈ 9.1× and the author thinks this understates it.

| Technique | What it is (exact numbers) | Transfers to UTTT? |
|---|---|---|
| **Playout cap randomisation** | Full search (cap N) on a fraction `p = 0.25` of turns, fast search (cap n) elsewhere, only full-search turns recorded; `(N, n) = (600, 100)` then `(1000, 200)`. Fast searches disable Dirichlet noise. Motivation: value training is data-limited (one noisy bit per game) and wants many cheap games; policy training wants deep searches. | Yes, directly. With Gumbel search (2.2) the same idea reappears as MiniZero's *progressive simulation*. |
| **Forced playouts + policy target pruning** | Each root child gets at least `n_forced = sqrt(k · P(c) · Σ N)` visits, `k = 2`; before recording the target, subtract the forced visits that PUCT would not have made on its own and drop children left with 1 visit. Decouples the *training target* from search/noise dynamics. | Yes if using PUCT+Dirichlet. Unnecessary under Gumbel, whose target is `softmax(logits + σ(completed Q))`, not visit counts. |
| **Global pooling** | Mean, width-scaled mean and max per channel (3c values) → FC → channel-wise bias added to another block; used in 2–3 residual blocks and the policy head. | Marginal: a 9×9 board is already inside the receptive field after 4 3×3 convs. Cheap to add; worth an ablation. |
| **Auxiliary opponent-reply policy** | Extra policy channel predicting next turn's target, loss weight 0.15. | Yes, nearly free. |
| **Ownership + score heads** | Ownership: per-point softmax over {own, opp, shared}, weight `1.5/b²`; score: categorical "pdf" loss (0.02) plus a "cdf" loss (0.02). Rationale: the game outcome is a function of finer sub-events; predicting them gives localised credit assignment. | Yes: **ownership = final status of each of the 9 local boards** (won-by-me / won-by-opp / drawn / unfinished), **score = final local-board-count margin** (−9…+9, 19 bins). Both are exact decompositions of the UTTT result, especially under a most-boards tiebreak. |
| Training loop | SGD mom. 0.9, batch 256, per-sample LR 6e-5 (3× lower for the first 5 M samples), growing window 250k → 22 M samples, SWA-style EMA of snapshots, gating at 100/200 wins. Value target is a 2-way softmax (win/loss), weight 1.5, L2 3e-5. Net grows 6×96 → 10×128 → 15×192 → 20×256 during the run. | Growing window and net-size schedule are sensible; gating is optional (see 2.6). |

Later KataGo additions documented in [KataGoMethods.md](https://github.com/lightvector/KataGo/blob/master/docs/KataGoMethods.md): no batch-norm (rare-activation pathologies also seen by Lc0), short-term value heads, "soft resignation" (finish resigned games with cheap searches to avoid biasing endgame data), uncertainty-weighted playouts, an "optimistic policy" head (+40–90 Elo at test time, not used in self-play).

### 2.2 Gumbel AlphaZero / Gumbel MuZero (Danihelka, Guez, Schrittwieser, Silver, ICLR 2022)

Sources: [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=bERaNdoegnO) (blocks bots; PDF mirror: [gumbel-alphazero.pdf](https://davidstarsilver.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/gumbel-alphazero.pdf)), [ICLR slides](https://iclr.cc/media/iclr-2022/Slides/6418.pdf), reference code in [mctx](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mctx).

- **Problem.** AlphaZero's visit-count target is only a policy improvement if *all* root actions are visited; with few simulations "MuZero fails to learn from 16 or less simulations" on 9×9 Go, whereas "Gumbel MuZero learns reliably even with 2 simulations" (paper Fig. 2; agents evaluated at 800 sims).
- **Root: Gumbel-Top-k + Sequential Halving.** Sample `m = min(n, 16)` actions *without replacement* by taking the top-m of `g(a) + logits(a)` with i.i.d. Gumbel `g`. Split the `n` simulations into `⌈log₂ m⌉` phases, visiting surviving actions equally and discarding the worse half each phase by `g + logits + σ(q̂)`. Final action = argmax of that score among the most-visited set (**deterministic**, minimises simple regret). Sampling `m = 4` learned slower; `m = 16` is the default.
- **σ transform.** `σ(q̂(a)) = (c_visit + max_b N(b)) · c_scale · q̂(a)` with **`c_visit = 50`, `c_scale = 1.0`** for Go/chess (insensitive for any `c_visit ≥ 50`); for Atari, Q-values normalised to [0,1] then `c_scale = 0.1`. mctx's default `qtransform_completed_by_mix_value` uses `maxvisit_init = 50`, `value_scale = 0.1`, `rescale_values = True` ([qtransforms.py](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mctx/blob/main/mctx/_src/qtransforms.py)); pgx trained with `value_scale = 0.1`, `rescale = False`.
- **Completed Q-values and the training target.** Unvisited actions get `v_mix = (v̂ + ΣN · Σ_{visited} π(a)q(a)/Σ_{visited} π(a)) / (1 + ΣN)` (Appendix D); improved policy `π' = softmax(logits + σ(completedQ))`; policy loss `KL(π', π)` trains *all* actions. This is the `action_weights` mctx returns.
- **Non-root nodes ("Full Gumbel").** Deterministic selection `argmin_a |π'(a) − N(a)/(1+ΣN)|`, i.e. drive the empirical visit distribution toward the improved policy — no PUCT constant to tune.
- **Why it helps small budgets.** The policy improvement guarantee holds for any `n ≥ 1`, so simulations can be cut 25–50× (800 → 16–32), which multiplies games/hour for the data-starved value head. Independent replications: pgx trains five games with **32 sims** (2.4); MiniZero finds Gumbel-AlphaZero with **n = 2** matching AlphaZero with n = 200 in 8×8 Othello *at equal wall-clock* (one iteration: 378.5 s at n=200 vs 40.6 s at n=16 vs 23.3 s at n=2), though at equal *iterations* n = 200 is stronger ([MiniZero §IV-C](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.11305v3)); a hobby chess engine reports "32 sims instead of 800 — roughly a 25× throughput multiplier, and the difference between this working on one machine and not" ([pfyhr/chessbot](https://github.com/pfyhr/chessbot)); a Gumbel-AlphaZero-on-pgx repo trains strong 9×9 Gomoku in 4–5 h on an RTX 4070 Ti ([zjjMaiMai/GumbelAlphaZero](https://github.com/zjjMaiMai/GumbelAlphaZero)).
- **Progressive simulation** (MiniZero §III-C): same total budget as fixed n = 16 but n grows from 2 to 200 over training; beats fixed-n on both 9×9 Go and Othello (+276 Elo jump in Othello once n exceeded 16), on the reasoning that search hurts while evaluations are bad and helps later.
- Gumbel still benefits from the first-30-move proportional sampling (paper Appendix F, Fig. 8b); use `gumbel_scale = 0` for evaluation.

### 2.3 Other algorithmic work, and whether it applies

- **EfficientZero** ([arXiv 2111.00210](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.00210)): 194 % mean human on Atari-100k via a self-supervised consistency loss on the learned dynamics, an LSTM "value prefix", and model-based off-policy correction. All three address a *learned* model; with an exact simulator only the idea of refreshing stale targets (reanalyse) carries over.
- **ReZero** ([arXiv 2404.16364](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16364)): backward-view reuse of child value estimates and periodic whole-buffer reanalysis to cut MuZero's reanalyse cost; board games included in the evaluation. Relevant only if we reanalyse.
- **Sampled MuZero** ([arXiv 2104.06303](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06303)): policy iteration over sampled action subsets for large/continuous action spaces. Irrelevant at 81 actions (and Gumbel subsumes the sampling idea).
- **Warm-start self-play** ([Wang et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12357)): rollouts/RAVE in the first iterations to replace a random network's evaluations. Same idea as utttai's stage-1 dataset from pure-MCTS play (2.5).
- **MCTS only at test time** ([Scheiermann & Konen 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.13307)): TD-trained n-tuple networks on CPU, MCTS wrapped around them only when playing; beats Othello's Edax up to level 7 — evidence that cheap value learning plus test-time search is a strong baseline. **Path-consistency regularisation** ([PCZero, ICML 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/zhao22h/zhao22h.pdf)) beats AlphaZero on 13×13 Hex with 900k games on 8 × 2080 Ti. **Athénan/Descent** claims large efficiency gains over Polygames' AlphaZero with minimax-style search and no policy head ([arXiv 2012.10700](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.10700)).
- **Search control** (start self-play from stored interesting states): Go-Exploit ([arXiv 2302.12359](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12359)), regret-guided variants ([arXiv 2602.20809](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20809)). Cheap to add later; UTTT's first-move symmetry-reduced opening space is tiny, so diversity of *mid-game* starts may matter.
- **Value target variants.** Oracle found training the value head on a mix of the game result `z` and the root search value `q` (average, or linear fall-off from z to q over 20 generations) learned faster than either alone on Connect Four ([part 4](https://medium.com/oracledevs/lessons-from-alphazero-part-4-improving-the-training-target-6efba2e71628)); utttai trained on the root's mean value only. An ALA-2020 study argues the on-policy z target is biased by exploration and proposes greedy backups (A0GB), with all off-policy variants beating vanilla on Connect Four and Breakthrough ([paper](https://ala2020.vub.ac.be/papers/ALA2020_paper_18.pdf)). KataGo's short-term value heads are the mature form of this.

### 2.4 Libraries and what they achieved in how long

- **mctx** (DeepMind, JAX): batched AlphaZero/MuZero/Gumbel policies, JIT-compiled, "defined for and operate on batches of inputs in parallel"; README recommends `gumbel_muzero_policy`. Example projects: pgx, a0-jax (Connect Four/Gomoku/Go), mctx-az (subtree persistence).
- **pgx** (Koyamada et al., NeurIPS 2023, [arXiv 2303.17503](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17503)): JAX game simulators, ≥ 10⁵ steps/s on one A100 even for the slowest env, 10–100× faster than PettingZoo/OpenSpiel at batch 1024. The AlphaZero example is **Gumbel AlphaZero with mctx**: 6 ResNet blocks × 128 channels, **32 simulations/move, 1024 vectorised envs, 256 self-play steps per iteration, mini-batch 4096, Adam 1e-3, 400 iterations ≈ 105 M frames**, no replay buffer (each iteration trains on its own data). Measured wall-clock (Table 23): **9×9 Go 8.6 h on one A100; Hex 11×11 17.6 h, Othello 8×8 11.4 h, Animal shogi 6.2 h on one RTX A4000; Gardner chess 14.3 h on 4 × A4000**. Self-play was > 90 % of the time; 8 GPUs gave ~4× wall-clock speed-up. The 9×9 Go baseline (800 sims) beat Pachi-10k 62–38.
- **MiniZero** (C++/PyTorch, [arXiv 2310.11305](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11305)): 3-block nets, 2,000 games/iteration, 200 optimiser steps per iteration, batch 1024, SGD 0.1, window 40k games, 300 iterations (600k games) on 4 × GTX 1080 Ti for 9×9 Go / 8×8 Othello.
- **AlphaZero.jl** ([Connect Four tutorial](https://jonathan-laurent.github.io/AlphaZero.jl/stable/tutorial/connect_four/)): 5 blocks × 128 filters (1.6 M params), 5,000 games/iteration at 600 sims, `c_puct = 2.0`, Dirichlet ε = 0.25, α = 1.0, τ = 1.0 until move 20 → 0.3 by move 30, buffer 400k → 1 M samples, Adam 2e-3, batch 1024, L2 1e-4, symmetry augmentation, position averaging, 15 iterations at 1–2 h each on an RTX 2070: beats depth-5 minimax with heuristic and makes few mistakes detectable within 14 plies, but "still imperfect at longer term strategical decisions". Notes that raising sims 300 → 600 sped learning despite the cost, and that tuning a Leela-style prior temperature helped.
- **turbozero_torch** ([lowrollr](https://github.com/lowrollr/turbozero_torch)): vectorised AlphaZero + environments in PyTorch, subtree persistence, 4,096 parallel envs on GPU; "completes similar tasks in minutes/hours" where process-parallel Python takes days/weeks (Othello, 2048 examples).
- **ARENA RL chapter 2.5** ([learn.arena.education](https://learn.arena.education/chapter2_rl/05_mcts_alphazero)): tree-as-tensors vectorised MCTS in PyTorch for Connect 4 scored against Pascal Pons's perfect solver; "trains to a strong level in under five minutes on a GPU".
- **alpha-zero-general** ([suragnair](https://github.com/suragnair/alpha-zero-general)): 6×6 Othello, 80 iterations × 100 episodes × 25 sims, **3 days on a Tesla K80** — the canonical example of a Python-per-node implementation being ~100× too slow.
- **AlphaZero_Gomoku** ([junxiaosong](https://github.com/junxiaosong/AlphaZero_Gomoku)): 6×6/4-in-row "reasonably good" in 500–1,000 games (~2 h on a PC); 8×8/5-in-row 2,000–3,000 games (~2 days); 400 sims, `c_puct = 5`, buffer 10k positions with 8× symmetry augmentation, KL-controlled adaptive LR.
- **michaelnny/alpha_zero** (PyTorch): 9×9 Go strong in ~40 h on 8 × 3090 with 10 blocks × 128 filters, > 1 M games; "a single RTX 3090 (24 GB) can accommodate approximately 20 actors"; 9×9 Gomoku strong "within a day or two" on one GPU with ≥ 32 actors at 200 sims ([repo](https://github.com/michaelnny/alpha_zero)).
- **Oracle Connect Four series** (2018): 7,168 games/generation (140–220k positions), 20 × 128 net, remote TensorRT-INT8 inference, virtual-loss parallel MCTS to reach > 95 % GPU utilisation; after tuning, ~40 generations and **21 GPU-hours** for a near-perfect player (from 77 h; ~450 h before engineering) ([part 5](https://medium.com/oracledevs/lessons-from-alphazero-part-5-performance-optimization-664b38dc509e), [part 6](https://medium.com/oracledevs/lessons-from-alpha-zero-part-6-hyperparameter-tuning-b1cfcbe4ca9a)).
- **Coding-agent benchmark** ([arXiv 2604.25067](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25067)): frontier agents implementing AlphaZero for Connect Four from a task description with **3 hours on a consumer single-GPU desktop**; the best runs beat the Pons solver as first mover in 7/8 trials. Useful calibration of what "3 GPU-hours" buys for a solved 4.5×10¹²-state game.

### 2.5 UTTT-specific prior work

- **utttai** ([ar-nowaczynski/utttai](https://github.com/ar-nowaczynski/utttai)): 5 M-parameter net designed for the browser; stage 1 trained on **8 M positions from pure-rollout MCTS self-play**, stage 2 on 8 M positions from neural-MCTS self-play; offline RL (self-play fully separated from training); "more MCTS simulations per position for training (quality over quantity)"; value target = root mean value; masked-KL policy loss; auxiliary action-value head; augmentation by flipping. Result: NMCTS2 at 1k sims beats rollout-MCTS at 10 M sims. Rollout MCTS throughput in single-threaded C++: 1 M rollouts in 5.0 s, 10 M in 51.4 s (i7-10700K). The [network file](https://github.com/ar-nowaczynski/utttai/blob/master/utttpy/selfplay/policy_value_network.py) uses a **3×3 stride-3 conv** as its first layer — i.e. it collapses each local board into a vector, then processes the 3×3 macro grid, then upsamples for the policy: a "3×3-of-3×3" design.
- **tacult** ([lunathanael/tacult](https://github.com/lunathanael/tacult)): PyTorch vectorised MCTS over hundreds of trees with a C++ bitboard engine (> 5,000 node evaluations/s); planes = X, O, valid-subgrid, side-to-move, 8 history frames; config shows 128 envs, `numMCTSSims 4000`, `cpuct 2.4`, arena gating at 60 %.
- Small student projects ([keeeal/alpha-ut3](https://github.com/keeeal/alpha-ut3), [Arnav Paruthi](https://arnavparuthi.medium.com/playing-ultimate-tic-tac-toe-using-reinforcement-learning-892f084f7def) with 600 sims and a 3-conv+2-dense net, [mlkg-dev](https://github.com/mlkg-dev/UTTT-AlphaGoZero) with ~40k games per generation) confirm feasibility but report no rigorous strength numbers.
- A DFPN/PN² solver exists ([minimax.dev](https://minimax.dev/docs/ultimate/)) — usable to label endgame test positions exactly, as Oracle did with the Connect Four solver.

### 2.6 Documented failure modes

- **Value-head overfitting / "catastrophic" value fit.** Leela Chess (no gating) saw the value head overfit and strength regress; dropping `value_loss_weight` to 0.25 reversed it. The Leela Zero maintainer's counter-view: overfitting means memorising games, and LR drops within a run are what invite it ([leela-zero #1480](https://github.com/leela-zero/leela-zero/issues/1480)). AlphaGo Zero itself used 0.01 value weight in the supervised setting to avoid it. KataGo doubled the window for its 600-playout ablation because of "major overfitting due to lack of data".
- **Policy collapse.** PUCT's `+1` denominator makes visit distributions sharper than the prior even with equal values, so a slightly under-valued move can be driven to ~0 policy and stay unexplored; Dirichlet noise alone does not prevent it ([KataGoMethods.md](https://github.com/lightvector/KataGo/blob/master/docs/KataGoMethods.md)). Remedies: root softmax temperature > 1, forced playouts + target pruning, or Gumbel's KL-to-improved-policy target.
- **Too-small or stale replay window.** Oracle saw a jump in learning at the moment the first (worst) generation aged out of a 20-generation window, and switched to a window growing from 4 to 20 generations; KataGo and AlphaZero.jl also grow the window. Conversely a window smaller than a few iterations overfits.
- **Duplicated positions / poor opening diversity.** 30–50 % duplicates in the Connect Four window; deduplication with target averaging helped (AlphaZero.jl's `use_position_averaging`). UTTT's first two plies are similar (few distinct openings after symmetry).
- **Learning rate / over-training.** A 12-hyperparameter study on small games found training "highly sensitive" and that *more* epochs or simulations per iteration can reduce strength; the number of outer self-play iterations dominates and the inner knobs (sims, episodes, epochs) should be kept low and scaled together ([Wang et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.05988)). AlphaZero_Gomoku controls the LR by targeting KL(old‖new) ≈ 0.02 per update.
- **Policy–value misalignment and symmetry inconsistency.** Value predictions can contradict the policy and vary across symmetric copies of a state, generalising badly to rarely-visited states, all masked by search; symmetry-targeted augmentation cut value generalisation error by ~50 % on small games ([VISA-VIS, arXiv 2301.11857](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.11857)). Symmetry augmentation therefore helps *if the symmetries are real* (see §3.4); a wrong "symmetry" corrupts targets.
- **Draw death.** In drawish games self-play converges to draws from move 1, starving the value head of signal and producing a player that cannot beat weaker opponents (chess discussion: [TalkChess](https://talkchess.com/forum3/viewtopic.php?t=75063&start=10); Lc0's [WDL head](https://lczero.org/blog/2020/04/wdl-head/) and contempt; [search-contempt](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07757)). Plain UTTT has drawn local boards and drawn games; the most-boards tiebreak reduces but does not remove draws. Monitor the draw rate per iteration.
- **Batch-norm blind spots** on rare activations (Lc0 promotion channels; KataGo seki) — one reason KataGo dropped BN.
- **Resignation bias**: resigning early removes endgame positions from the data; AlphaGo Zero keeps 10 % of games un-resigned; KataGo "soft-resigns". UTTT games are ≤ 81 plies, so simply do not resign.

## 3. Design recommendations specific to UTTT

### 3.1 Input planes (9×9, current-player perspective)

1. own marks, 2. opponent marks, 3. local boards won by me (broadcast over the board's 9 cells), 4. won by opponent, 5. drawn/full local boards, 6. **legal-move mask** (equivalently the "active board" plane; it encodes the send rule, which is the only non-Markov-looking part of the state), 7. constant plane = 1 if I am the first player (first-move advantage), optionally 8. a constant-ones plane (border detection under zero padding). No history planes: the UTTT state plus the active-board constraint is Markov, and tacult's 8-frame history is redundant. Always present the board from the mover's perspective (AlphaGo Zero's colour-transposition trick), which halves what the net must learn.

### 3.2 Architecture choice

- **9×9 conv ResNet** (default): 4–8 residual blocks × 64–128 filters (~0.3–1.5 M params), 3×3 kernels; four blocks already give every cell a full-board receptive field. It is what pgx/MiniZero/AlphaZero.jl use for boards of this size, and Ampere tensor cores make a 64–128-filter 9×9 forward pass on a 1024-batch take single-digit milliseconds. Add KataGo-style global pooling or a squeeze-excite block once, as an ablation.
- **3×3-of-3×3 structured net** (utttai): stride-3 3×3 conv to embed each local board, 1×1/3×3 convs on the 3×3 macro grid, transposed-conv back to 9×9 for the policy. Matches the game's hierarchy and is very cheap; risk is losing cross-board "same cell index" relations that the send rule creates (a mark at micro-cell c in any board sends the opponent to board c). A plain 9×9 conv with stride 1 sees those relations only through depth; a hybrid (9×9 trunk + a macro-level branch) is a reasonable experiment.
- **Flat MLP** (81×k inputs): works for tic-tac-toe-sized games and is the fastest to evaluate, but has no weight sharing across boards; expect worse sample efficiency. Fine for a first end-to-end smoke test.
- **Small transformer** over 81 tokens (or 9 board tokens + 81 cell tokens) with learned positional embeddings: attention can express "cell c in every board ↔ board c" directly; recent Go work shows transformers can bridge local/global knowledge ([arXiv 2410.05347](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.05347)), but at this scale a ResNet is the safer baseline. Try only after the conv baseline is solid.

### 3.3 Heads and targets

- **Policy:** 81 logits, illegal moves masked to −∞ before softmax, trained with CE against the search target (visit distribution under PUCT, `π'` under Gumbel). Add KataGo's **opponent-reply auxiliary policy** (weight 0.15).
- **Value: 3-way win/draw/loss softmax** rather than a tanh scalar. Draws exist (and are frequent in plain rules); Lc0 argues 0.5 from a scalar cannot distinguish "certain draw" from "50/50" and the WDL head trains with draw information directly ([Lc0 WDL post](https://lczero.org/blog/2020/04/wdl-head/)); KataGo's value head is a softmax over outcomes. For search use `v = P(win) − P(loss)`.
- **Auxiliary heads (KataGo transfer):** *ownership* = for each of the 9 local boards a 4-way softmax {mine, opponent's, drawn, unfinished-at-game-end} (weight ~0.5 total, i.e. ~0.05 per board); *score* = final board-count margin as a 19-bin categorical (−9…+9) with a small weight (0.02–0.1) plus KataGo's cdf-style loss. Under the most-boards tiebreak the margin *is* the outcome decomposition; without the tiebreak it still localises credit ("which board did I lose?"). These cost nothing at inference and the KataGo ablation (1.65×) is the strongest single effect in that paper.
- **Value target:** start with `z`; consider Oracle's z→q fall-off or KataGo-style short-horizon search-value heads once the pipeline is stable.

### 3.4 Symmetry group (verified)

The automorphism group of UTTT is the **dihedral group of the square, D₄ (order 8), acting simultaneously on the macro grid and on every micro grid**. Argument: a symmetry must map the 81 cells to cells, preserve membership in local boards, preserve the 8 lines of every local board and of the macro board, and preserve the send rule. Preserving local boards and lines forces the map to act as some `g ∈ D₄` on board indices and as some `h_b ∈ D₄` inside each board `b`. The send rule ("a mark at micro cell c sends the opponent to board c") maps to "a mark at `h_b(c)` sends to board `g(c)`", which is a rule of the same game only if `h_b(c) = g(c)` for all b, c — so every `h_b = g`. Hence exactly the 8 elements `{rot⁰, rot⁹⁰, rot¹⁸⁰, rot²⁷⁰} × {identity, reflection}` applied identically at both levels. The same "rotate/reflect the outer board and each inner field together" action is formalised (with a proof that legal games map to legal games) in [Group actions on winning games of super tic-tac-toe, arXiv 1606.04779](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04779) (note: that paper's ring-rotation generalisation for larger n is not line-preserving; for 3×3 the relevant group is D₄).

- **Swapping micro and macro coordinates, `(b, c) ↦ (c, b)`, is NOT a symmetry.** It maps a local-board line (three cells in one board) to three cells at the same index in three different boards, which is not a winning pattern; and it maps "sent to board c" to "sent to board b", breaking the send rule. Do not augment with it.
- **Colour swap** is handled by the current-player encoding, not as a spatial transform.
- Implementation: apply one random `g` per sample at training time (and, AlphaGo-Zero style, at self-play inference) by permuting the 81 policy entries and the 9 ownership entries consistently; do not store 8 copies in the buffer. utttai's "flipping" used only a subset of the group; using all 8 is correct.
- Symmetry also shrinks the opening: the 81 first moves fall into **15 orbits** under D₄ (Burnside: identity fixes 81 cells, each rotation fixes only the centre-of-centre cell, each of the four reflections fixes 3×3 = 9 cells; (81 + 3 + 36)/8 = 15); transposition-style deduplication of the first few plies is cheap and reduces duplicate-position waste.

## 4. Batched / vectorised MCTS on the GPU

### 4.1 How mctx + pgx do it

The tree is a fixed-size array per game (`[B, num_simulations+1]` nodes with child-index, visit, value and prior tables); a simulation step is executed in lock-step for all `B` games: vectorised selection down the tree (a `while_loop` over depth), one batched environment step (pgx's pure-JAX `step` on `[B]` states), **one network forward for all B leaves**, and a vectorised backup. Everything is `jit`-compiled, so per-simulation overhead is a handful of fused kernels and self-play throughput scales with B until the GPU saturates (pgx: ≥10⁵ env steps/s at B = 1024 on an A100; 8 GPUs ≈ 7.4× throughput). Costs: every game runs the same number of simulations (no early stopping), the tree size is fixed (no subtree reuse in mctx; turbozero and mctx-az add persistence), and dynamic control flow must be expressed as masks. The whole loop is one process, one GPU, no inter-process communication.

### 4.2 The alternative: CPU trees with a GPU inference server

Classic AlphaGo Zero/Lc0/KataGo/AlphaZero.jl design: many CPU search threads or processes each own a tree; leaves are gathered into mini-batches (AGZ: 8 per thread; Lc0: "set as large as your GPU can handle", tracking *collisions* when parallel selections hit the same leaf, [Lc0 technical explanation](https://lczero.org/dev/wiki/technical-explanation-of-leela-chess-zero/)); **virtual loss** discourages threads from co-selecting a path. Oracle needed both cross-thread evaluation caching (> 2× fewer net calls) and virtual-loss parallel MCTS to hit > 95 % GPU utilisation ([part 5](https://medium.com/oracledevs/lessons-from-alphazero-part-5-performance-optimization-664b38dc509e)). The trade-off is search quality: leaf parallelisation "lacks diversity … collapse of exploration", virtual loss "could cause exploitation failure"; WU-UCT's "unobserved samples" statistic gets near-linear speed-up with limited loss ([Liu et al., ICLR 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.11755)). With Gumbel's Sequential Halving the root visits are pre-planned, so batching the root's phase across games is natural and virtual loss is only needed inside subtrees.

On this machine the CPU route is hampered by the lack of a C++ compiler: a Python-per-node tree runs ~10³–10⁴ nodes/s per core (alpha-zero-general took 3 days on a K80 for 6×6 Othello at 25 sims), and Windows multiprocessing must use `spawn` (no `fork`), so each worker re-imports PyTorch and cannot share CUDA state ([pytorch #77159](https://github.com/pytorch/pytorch/issues/77159)). **Numba** needs no compiler — LLVM is statically linked into the `llvmlite` wheel ([Numba install docs](https://numba.readthedocs.io/en/stable/user/installing.html)) — so a bitboard UTTT engine and even a per-process tree can be JIT-compiled to C-like speed; 12 cores × Numba trees + a batched GPU server is a viable second design if the lock-step GPU design proves awkward.

### 4.3 What is achievable in PyTorch on Windows

- **Lock-step vectorised MCTS in pure PyTorch** (turbozero_torch, ARENA, tacult) is the closest analogue to mctx. Expect per-simulation overhead of tens of small kernel launches (selection over depth, gather/scatter for expansion and backup); with small nets this launch overhead, not FLOPs, bounds throughput. Mitigations: keep everything on-device (no `.item()`/host syncs), large B (1,024–8,192 games; memory is trivial for UTTT), bounded tree depth, `torch.cuda.graphs` for the fixed-shape network forward (works natively on Windows), fp16/bf16 autocast and `channels_last` on Ampere.
- **`torch.compile`** on Windows CUDA works through the community **triton-windows** wheels (Ampere sm86 supported, bundled minimal CUDA toolchain and TinyCC so no MSVC is needed; pin the Triton minor to the PyTorch minor: 2.7↔3.3, 2.8↔3.4, 2.9↔3.5, 2.10↔3.6) ([triton-lang/triton-windows](https://github.com/triton-lang/triton-windows), [README](https://github.com/woct0rdho/triton-windows)). It fuses the small elementwise/gather kernels of the tree ops, but treat it as an optimisation to try after the plain version works; the official PyTorch position is that Windows inductor support is CPU/XPU-oriented ([PyTorch tutorial](https://docs.pytorch.org/tutorials/unstable/inductor_windows.html)).
- **Two GPUs:** run self-play on the 3090 and training on the 3060 (or a second self-play process on the 3060 — its ~1/3 throughput is still worth having), exchanging weights every iteration via files; a 24 GB card can also hold several self-play processes if the design is CPU-bound.
- **Ball-park throughput for UTTT** (assumptions stated): 6×64 net ≈ 80 MFLOPs/position → ~4–8 ms per 1,024-leaf forward in fp16; 32 simulations/move → ~0.15–0.3 s of network time per lock-step move for 1,024 games, plus comparable tree-op overhead; ~45 plies/game → ~10–25 s per 1,024 games → **~40–100 games/s ≈ 150k–350k games/hour**, i.e. 1 M games in 3–7 h if the tree ops are well vectorised, several times slower if not. Anchors: pgx reaches ≈1.5–2 M Othello/Hex games in 11–18 h on an A4000 (~½ a 3090) in JAX; ARENA's Connect 4 trains in < 5 min; Oracle's tuned Connect Four run was 21 GPU-hours on 2018 hardware.

## 5. Recommended starting hyperparameters (with justification)

- **Search: Gumbel AlphaZero, n = 32 simulations, m = 16 sampled actions, c_visit = 50, c_scale = 1.0, completed-Q with mixed value, Full-Gumbel non-root selection.** Justification: guaranteed improvement at small n (paper), pgx trains five board games at exactly this setting, MiniZero shows equal-wall-clock parity with n = 200 PUCT; 81 ≥ m so sampling matters on the "free move" turns and degenerates to exhaustive visits on ≤9-move turns (where AlphaZero's guarantee already holds). Progressive simulation 16 → 64–128 in the last third of training (MiniZero). Keep a PUCT+Dirichlet path (`c_init 1.25–2.0`, α = 1.0, ε = 0.25, forced playouts k = 2 + target pruning) for A/B comparison.
- **Exploration:** Gumbel noise at the root (scale 1.0 in self-play, 0 in evaluation) plus proportional-to-visit sampling for the first 8–12 plies (AlphaZero's 30 of ~100+ plies scaled to 45-ply games; Oracle found more sampling helped in Connect Four, so this is a knob to widen, not narrow).
- **Network:** 6 blocks × 64 filters ResNet (≈0.5 M params) with the heads of §3.3; grow to 8×128 only if the loss plateaus while the buffer still has signal (KataGo/Leela grow nets mid-run). No batch-norm if instability appears (KataGo).
- **Self-play batch:** 1,024–4,096 concurrent games (pgx 1024; turbozero 4096).
- **Data per iteration and buffer:** 1,024 envs × 128–256 steps ≈ 130k–260k positions/iteration; growing window from ~4 to ~20 iterations (≈3–5 M positions ≈ 4–5 GB in RAM; KataGo 250k→22 M, Oracle 4→20 generations, AlphaZero.jl 400k→1 M).
- **Optimisation:** SGD momentum 0.9, LR 0.02 for batch 1024 (AlphaZero 0.2 at batch 4096 ≈ same per-sample rate; MiniZero 0.1 at 1024 with 3 blocks), weight decay 1e-4, or Adam 1e-3 (pgx, AlphaZero.jl) as the forgiving alternative; ~1–2 passes over each iteration's new data (Oracle: 2 epochs; MiniZero: ~1.7 samples/position; Wang et al.: do not over-train); cosine/1-cycle within an iteration optional (Oracle). Loss weights: policy 1, WDL 1 (drop to 0.25–0.5 if the value loss keeps falling while strength stalls — Lc0), ownership 0.5, score 0.05, opponent-policy 0.15.
- **Augmentation:** random D₄ element per sample (§3.4), and at self-play inference.
- **Gating:** none (AlphaZero, pgx, MiniZero); instead evaluate every iteration against fixed anchors — random, rollout-MCTS at 10k/100k rollouts (utttai's C++ numbers imply ~200k rollouts/s; a Numba engine will be slower), previous checkpoints (BayesElo) — and a **solver-labelled endgame test set** (minimax.dev's DFPN solver) for a mistake-rate metric like Oracle's. Turn 55 % gating on only if Elo oscillates.
- **Resignation:** off. **Draw handling:** log draw rate; if it climbs above ~50 % under plain rules, add contempt or switch to the most-boards tiebreak early.

## Key takeaways for our project

1. Search is the engine: even AlphaGo Zero's final raw policy is ~2,000 Elo below the searched player; a small net plus search is the target, not a big net.
2. The value head is the data-starved part (one noisy label per game); everything that helps at small scale — playout-cap randomisation, few-sim Gumbel search, auxiliary ownership/score heads, mixing q into z — attacks that bottleneck.
3. Use Gumbel AlphaZero at 32 simulations; it is the single change that makes one-GPU training work in the published replications, and it removes c_puct, Dirichlet α and target pruning from the tuning list.
4. Transfer KataGo's auxiliary targets literally: ownership of the 9 local boards and the board-count margin; they were KataGo's largest ablation effect and are exact sub-events of the UTTT result.
5. Use a 3-way WDL value head; draws are real in UTTT and drawish self-play ("draw death") is a documented failure mode to monitor.
6. The symmetry group is D₄ acting on macro and micro grids together (8 elements). Micro/macro transposition is not a symmetry; using it would poison targets.
7. Lock-step vectorised MCTS on the GPU (mctx/pgx style, in PyTorch as turbozero/ARENA do) avoids Windows' compiler and `spawn` limitations; Numba is the escape hatch for any CPU-side engine.
8. Throughput is bounded by kernel-launch overhead, not FLOPs: batch thousands of games, avoid host syncs, use CUDA graphs; torch.compile is available on Windows via triton-windows but is an optimisation, not a prerequisite.
9. Grow the replay window (4 → 20 iterations) and do not over-train per iteration; the outer loop count matters most.
10. Skip gating (AlphaZero, pgx, MiniZero) but build the evaluation harness first: rollout-MCTS anchors, checkpoint ladders, and a solver-labelled endgame set give an objective mistake rate.
11. Do not resign, do not add history planes, do not replicate 8 symmetric copies in the buffer; do apply a random symmetry per sample.
12. Calibrated expectation: Connect Four reaches near-perfect play in ~3–20 GPU-hours on consumer hardware; UTTT is larger, so plan for hundreds of thousands to a couple of million games and a day or two of 3090 time for a strong agent, with the first meaningful signal within an hour.
